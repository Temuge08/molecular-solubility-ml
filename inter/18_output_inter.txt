What are the three stages doing?
1. Hard filtering

Before ranking, we reject molecules that don't meet basic criteria:

QED >= 0.5
Lipinski violations <= 1
inside ESOL model descriptor range

This prevents a molecule from compensating for a serious problem just because another score is excellent.

For example:

QED = 0.9
but
outside solubility model domain

shouldn't automatically rank first.

2. Multi-objective score

We currently use:

45% QED
35% predicted solubility
20% Lipinski compliance

The important part isn't these exact numbers.

The important idea is:

Normalize different objectives first, then explicitly define their relative importance.

We're using percentile scores so:

QED
predicted logS

both become approximately:

0 → worst among candidates
1 → best among candidates

before combining them.

These weights are generic because we don't have a specific therapeutic target yet.

3. Pareto front

This is more interesting than the weighted score.

Imagine:

             QED ↑

Candidate A    0.95     logS -4.0
Candidate B    0.85     logS -2.5
Candidate C    0.65     logS -1.5

There isn't necessarily one objectively best molecule.

A has better drug-likeness.

C has better predicted solubility.

B compromises.

All three might be Pareto optimal.

A molecule is dominated if another candidate is at least as good in every objective and better in at least one.

This is very relevant to drug discovery because molecular optimization is almost always multi-objective.

4. Why diversity selection?

Suppose your top 100 ranked candidates look like:

Molecule A
Molecule A + CH3
Molecule A + Cl
Molecule A + F
Molecule A + OH
...

You technically have 100 molecules, but chemically they're one idea.

Instead we use:

Morgan fingerprint
       ↓
Tanimoto similarity
       ↓
reject if similarity > 0.65
to an already-selected molecule

giving:

Candidate 1 → scaffold/family A
Candidate 2 → family B
Candidate 3 → family C
...

This is much closer to what you'd want before downstream experimental or docking work.
